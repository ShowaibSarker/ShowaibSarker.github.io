Why this case

What you are actually learning

In the first case you worked out the cost of capital for a single project. This time you take on a whole company, and you do it the way an analyst actually does: starting from the filings, building a model, and ending with a recommendation you have to defend out loud.

Three questions, one thread

How is this company financed and what does that cost? What is it worth? Should we buy it? Each answer feeds the next. The discount rate you build in Part A is the rate you value with in Part B and the rate you price synergies with in Part C. Get Part A wrong and everything downstream moves.

Real data, real judgment

Every input comes from an SEC filing, a bond quote page, or a published rate. Nothing is handed to you except the projection assumptions, and you test those. The grade is not for arithmetic. It is for whether your conclusion survives questions.

Before you start. We work the whole method together in class on a different company first. Bring that worked example with you; the steps are identical.

The situation

Who you are and what the board wants

Stanley Black & Decker makes hand tools, power tools, and outdoor equipment under the DeWalt, Stanley, Craftsman, and Black+Decker brands, and sells engineered fastening systems to automotive and aerospace customers. It is headquartered in New Britain, Connecticut, has paid a dividend every year since 1877, and carries a BBB investment-grade credit rating. It uses debt in a meaningful way, and its capital structure is yours to uncover from its filings. Net sales have declined in recent years, and management is running a multi-year cost-reduction program it says will rebuild the operating margin. Tariff costs on imported products are the main open question for 2026.

Your team works in the corporate development group of a large industrial company. The board has asked you for three things, and they belong together.

1. How is SWK financed, and what would more leverage do?
Build the market value balance sheet, estimate the cost of equity, the cost of debt, the WACC, and the unlevered cost of capital. Then value a $1,000 million expansion and work out whether financing it with equity or with debt leaves shareholders better off, and by how much per share. Finish with the uncomfortable case: a project that creates value for the firm but is voted down because the lenders capture too much of it.
2. What is the company worth?
Project five years of cash flow from assets on the assumptions given, value the firm twice (once at the WACC and once by APV), and test how much the answer moves when the margin and the discount rate change. Then say plainly whether the stock is cheap, fair, or expensive, and what would have to be true for the market to be right instead of you.
3. Should we buy it, and at what price?
Value the synergies, work out the maximum price you could justify, and compare it with the offer on the table. Then decide. The interesting number is not the deal NPV; it is how much of the synergy value the premium hands to the target's shareholders before your firm sees a cent.

Every team values the same company from the same filings, so the results are comparable and are graded against one answer key with acceptable ranges for live market data. What differs across teams is the quality of the judgment.

Data rules

Read this before you open Excel

Filings
Use the most recent Form 10-Q for the balance-sheet numbers and the most recent Form 10-K for the three-year history, the long-term debt schedule, and the cash flow statement. Both are on sec.gov under Stanley Black & Decker, CIK 93556. Name the filings you used, with their filing dates, in the report.

Watch the current maturities line. The figure in the 10-K is much larger than the one in the 10-Q because notes were repaid in between. Using the wrong one is the single most common error in this case.
Market data and your data date
Pick one data date and use it for everything. Collect the share price, shares outstanding, beta, dividend, the bond quotes, and the 3-month Treasury yield as of that date. State the date in the report and keep a dated screenshot of every page in the appendix.

Price, shares, beta, dividend: Yahoo Finance or stockanalysis.com. Bond prices and yields: FINRA, Corporate and Agency Bonds, issuer "Stanley Black", recording the trade date shown, because a note may not trade every day. If FINRA shows no trade in the prior five business days, use the TradingView SWK bonds page and say so. A small issue with no quote at all is carried at par with the yield of the nearest quoted maturity, and you state that. The 3-month yield comes from the Daily Treasury Par Yield Curve at home.treasury.gov.
Conventions
Risk-free rate is the 3-month Treasury yield. The equity risk premium is the current implied premium from Aswath Damodaran's page at pages.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar, stated as of your data date. Tax rate is the 21 percent federal statutory rate. Use the published five-year beta.

SWK's income statement has no operating profit line, so build EBIT as earnings before income taxes plus interest expense. Take depreciation and amortization as depreciation of property, plant and equipment plus amortization of intangibles from the cash flow statement.
Using AI, and the one rule that matters
AI is required on this case study. Use it where an analyst would: finding items in the filings, drafting assumptions you then test, checking a formula, stress-testing your recommendation, building your dashboard, editing your writing.

AI output is never a source. When a tool gives you a number, open the filing or the quote page, find it yourself, screenshot it, and only then type it into your workbook. Where the two disagree, the filing wins, and that disagreement belongs in your AI Use Log. Full credit on the log requires at least one case where you caught an AI mistake and corrected it.
Everything traces to a cell. Build the model in the posted Excel template. Blue cells are inputs you retrieve. Yellow cells are formulas you write. If a number appears in your report, in your dashboard, or in your presentation, it must come from a cell in that workbook.

Part A. Capital structure and a leveraged expansion

Six questions

1. Read the balance sheet and list every note
From the 10-Q balance sheet, record cash, total current assets, total current liabilities, long-term debt, notes payable and current maturities of long-term debt, and total shareholders' equity. From the 10-K debt schedule, list every note still outstanding on your data date with its coupon, maturity, and face amount, and treat the current maturities and other debt as one line at par. Then compute the book value of total debt and the book debt-to-equity ratio.

This is the part people rush. Every later number depends on getting the debt list right.
2. Build the market value balance sheet
Report the market value of equity, the market value of debt, firm value, the weight of debt, the weight of equity, and the market debt-to-equity ratio. Compare it with the book ratio in one paragraph.

The comparison is the point. Book equity is historical cost; market equity is what the business is actually worth today. Any claim about how levered this company is has to be made on market values.
3. Find the cost of capital, then strip the leverage out of it
Estimate the cost of equity, the pre-tax and after-tax cost of debt, and the WACC on market-value weights. From these, recover the unlevered cost of capital R_0.

Then write the paragraph that matters: what drives the WACC, separating business risk from the use of debt and the tax shield, and why a lower WACC does not by itself make a company the better investment. A firm can lower its reported WACC by borrowing more without becoming one bit better at making tools.
4. Value a $1,000 million expansion, two ways
Management is weighing an expansion of the DeWalt professional line and the North American manufacturing footprint. It costs $1,000 million and is expected to raise pretax operating earnings by $130 million a year in perpetuity, at the same business risk as the firm.

(a) Find the NPV. (b) If it is financed with new equity, show the market value balance sheet after the announcement and report the new price per share and the number of shares to be issued. (c) If it is financed entirely with new perpetual debt, show the balance sheet including the interest tax shield and report the price per share. (d) State which financing gives the higher share price and by how much.

Two traps live here. The project is valued before its financing is chosen, and the new shares are sold after the market has already reacted to the announcement.
5. Work out how far the debt should go
After the debt-financed expansion, report the debt-to-equity ratio, the cost of equity, and the WACC, and explain why the cost of equity is higher than under all-equity financing.

Then, in one paragraph, use the costs of financial distress and the agency costs of debt to explain what actually limits SWK's borrowing, and say whether the company looks over-levered or under-levered today. Either verdict earns full marks if you argue it from the numbers and from the business.
6. When a good project still gets voted down
Suppose the expansion goes ahead and, because it makes the existing notes safer, the current lenders capture $300 million of the value created without putting in any new money. What NPV is left for the shareholders, and would they still approve it?

Then explain in one short paragraph why the split of value between lenders and shareholders, not the project NPV alone, decides the vote. This is the agency cost of debt in its clearest form.

Part B. What is SWK worth

Five questions, one verdict

Project five years of cash flow from assets from the most recent fiscal-year base, using the assumptions below. Every team uses the same set. These are given in the case, and you test them in the sensitivity analysis.

AssumptionBase case
Net sales growth, years 1 to 52.0% per year
Operating margin (EBIT proxy / net sales)10%
Depreciation and amortization3.4% of sales
Capital expenditures1.9% of sales
Investment in net working capital15% of the change in sales
Terminal growth after year 52.0%
Tax rate21%
7. Build five years of cash flow from assets
Start from EBIT, take out tax, add back depreciation and amortization, then subtract what the firm spends on equipment and on working capital. What is left is the cash the business can hand to everyone who financed it.

Formula CFA = EBIT × (1 − T) + D&A − CapEx − change in NWC
8. Value the firm with the WACC method
Discount the five flows and a terminal value at your WACC, move from enterprise value to equity value, and report the value per share against the market price on your data date.

Formula TV₅ = CFA₅(1 + g) / (WACC − g), then Equity = EV + cash − debt
9. Value it again with APV, and explain the gap
Discount the same cash flows at R_0 to get the value with no leverage, then add the tax shield on the existing debt separately. Report the value per share.

In one paragraph, say why the two methods differ a little and which assumption drives the gap. The WACC method assumes the capital structure stays at today's proportions forever; APV holds the dollar amount of debt fixed. For a lightly levered firm they barely disagree. For a heavily levered one they can.
10. Test the assumptions
Build a 3 by 3 table of value per share, varying the operating margin across 8, 10, and 12 percent and the WACC one point below, at, and one point above your estimate.

Then read it: what does the market price imply about the margin and the discount rate investors are using? The grid usually shows that a point on the discount rate moves the answer more than two points of margin, which tells you where to spend your attention.
11. Give the verdict
State SWK's fair value per share, compare it with the market price, and say whether the stock is cheap, fair, or expensive.

Then do the hard part: say what would have to be true about SWK for the market price to be right instead of your model. A team that declares the stock undervalued without confronting that question has not finished the analysis.

Part C. The acquisition

Four questions

Your company is evaluating an all-cash acquisition of SWK at a premium of 30 percent over the market price on your data date. Your operating team believes the combination would produce pretax synergies of $400 million a year, starting in year 1 and growing at the terminal rate, from savings in overlapping industrial distribution, procurement, and overhead. The premium and the synergies are assumptions given for the analysis, not estimates of what your company would actually pay.

12. Price the offer
Find the offer price per share and the total cost of the deal. Treat SWK's standalone value to its own shareholders as its market value of equity on your data date, and report your Part B value beside it for comparison.

An acquirer has to pay the market for the shares whatever its own model says, which is why the market value, not your DCF, is the base the premium sits on.
13. Value the synergies and find your ceiling
Value the synergies to your company, add them to SWK's standalone value, and report the maximum price per share you could justify and the maximum premium that implies.

Formula PV of synergies = after-tax synergy / (WACC − g); maximum price = (target equity + PV of synergies) / shares

Notice what the perpetuity assumes: that the savings are permanent and begin immediately. Neither is usually true.
14. Decide, then look at who actually wins
Find the NPV of the deal at the offered premium and say whether your company should proceed.

Then compute how much of the synergy value the 30 percent premium hands to SWK's shareholders. That number is usually the most revealing one in the whole case: the premium is paid up front and with certainty, while the synergies arrive later and only if your management executes.
15. Write the recommendation
Proceed, proceed at a lower premium (state it), or walk away. Say who bears the risk that the synergies do not appear, and why a cash offer puts that risk where it does. Two to three paragraphs, in your own words.

Close with one paragraph of limitations: integration cost and the time synergies take to arrive, antitrust review, how a deal of this size would be financed and what it would do to your company's own credit rating, and the assumption of SWK's debt. State these. Do not model them.

The presentation

Eight minutes, from your dashboard, not from slides

Every team presents in class. You open your dashboard, walk the room through the headline numbers, and give your recommendation. When you are asked about a figure, you click it, and the breakdown behind it appears on the screen. That is the whole point of the format: you should never be in a position where a number on your screen is one you cannot explain.

How to use the eight minutes

Roughly two minutes on how the company is financed and what its cost of capital is, two on what it is worth and why, two on the deal and your ceiling, and two on your recommendation and the risk that would change your mind. Do not read the dashboard aloud. Explain what it means.

Expect questions on any number

Questions go to whichever team member is standing there, not to the one who built that sheet. Everyone should be able to say where a figure came from, what it assumes, and what happens to the recommendation if the assumption is wrong.

Formulas you will need

All of them. There is nothing else hidden in the case.

Cost of capital

R_S = R_f + beta (R_M − R_f)
WACC = [S / (B + S)] R_S + [B / (B + S)] R_B (1 − T)
R_S = R_0 + (B/S)(1 − T)(R_0 − R_B)   solve backward for R_0

The expansion

PV = EBIT (1 − T) / R_0    V_L = V_U + T × B

Valuation

Cash flow from assets = EBIT (1 − T) + D&A − CapEx − change in NWC
Terminal value at year 5 = CFA₅ (1 + g) / (WACC − g)
Equity value = Enterprise value + cash − market value of debt
APV: unlevered value at R_0, then add T × B

The deal

PV of synergies = after-tax synergy / (WACC − g)
Maximum price per share = (target equity + PV of synergies) / shares
Deal NPV = (maximum price − offer price) × shares
